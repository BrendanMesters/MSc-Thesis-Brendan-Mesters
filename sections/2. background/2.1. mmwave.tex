\section{Millimetre Wave Radar}
\label{section: background - millimetre wave radar}

Millimetre Wave Radars (mmWave radar) are a type of sensor which has been discussed for many decades already, with early research dating back to the 1970s.
Commercial usage dates back to the 1990s for the telecommunication sector, with the automotive industry starting to use it at the turn of the century \cite{menzel2008years}.
In recent years, mmWave radars have been used in a broad number of sensing applications, from human tracking and activity recognition, through to object inspection and classification \cite[Fig.~2]{vanberlo2021millimeter}.

This recent interest in mmWave-based sensing solutions over some of the alternative methods, such as LiDAR and camera-based sensing methods, is due to some of the inherent advantages present in mmWave-based systems.
% vs cameras
Cameras, for example, provide a detailed, high-resolution image, but they are susceptible to environmental changes in the light intensity.
mmWave radars do not suffer from this limitation, since they are an active sensor, meaning that they observe the reflection of a signal which has been sent out by the sensor itself.
Furthermore, most cameras do not include any depth information; they produce a 2-D projection of a scene.
mmWave radars, on the other hand, provide a 3-D point cloud of the scene, which does have this range data.
% vs LiDAR
% \ednote{don't forget another difference, LiDAR does not have the same penetration}
mmWave radars share many of the previously mentioned attributes with LiDAR and radio-frequency radars.
The main differences between LiDAR and mmWave radars are the cost and point density.
Lidar is significantly more expensive, but also produces significantly denser point clouds, see \cref{figure: point density - lidar vs mmwave}.
An essential difference between LiDAR and mmWave radars, however, is cost.
The high cost of LiDAR is a serious barrier when it comes to broad adoption in IoT sensors.
% The high cost of LiDAR makes it an option for an everyday interface.
Other radio frequency radars suffer from lower accuracy due to the wavelength of the transmitted signal, which is longer than that of mmWave radars \cite{yan2025advancements}.
This results in a lower resolution 
As a result, mmWave radars are a cost-effective sensor for this job.

To achieve this, a mmWave radar transmits a millimetre wave of continuously increasing frequency, and by observing the backscatter, it can infer information about the scene.
% mmWave radars achieve this all in a very clever manner, namely, they send out a wave of constantly increasing frequency and observe the reflection.
% \ednote{This could maybe be explained more broadly}
Since the frequency is continuously increasing, we know that the distance to the object which caused the backscatter is proportional to the difference in frequency between the outgoing and incoming signals.
% The distance to the object which caused the backscatter is proportional to the difference in frequency between the outgoing and incoming signals, due to the continuously increasing frequency.
% Since the frequency is increasing at a constant rate, the distance to the object at which the wave is reflected is proportional to the difference in frequency between the outgoing and incoming signals.
An alternative name for these sensors is thus a \textit{Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave} (FMCW) sensor.
The mmWave radar uses a component called a \textit{mixer} to calculate the difference between the ongoing and outgoing signals, which takes two signals as inputs and provides the difference in frequency as well as the difference in phase between those two signals.
This frequency difference holds information about the macro-scale distance, while the phase difference gives information on the micro-scale movements of an object, which is very useful in some specific applications.

% \ednote{IF TIME PERMITS IT\\Shorten these paragraphs, they go into too much technical detail, which is not needed for this thesis.}

The mmWave radar has multiple receivers and multiple senders, which allows the sensor to calculate an elevation and an azimuth for each object.
These different receivers are spaced half a wavelength apart from each other.
This change in position means that the signal will have travelled a different distance, measurable in less than one wavelength, to each of the receivers.
This phase difference can be used to calculate a difference in distance to the object in question, which in turn can be used to calculate the angle of arrival.
The specific layout of Transmitters (tx) and Receivers (rx) on the mmWave radar (see \cref{fig:mmwave_radar_antennas}) means that the mmWave radar can calculate the elevation, azimuth, distance, and Doppler information (which tells us the velocity towards or away from the sensor) of objects in the scene, which is given to the user as a 4-D Point cloud.
A lot of technical details have been omitted in this explanation, as they are not very important to the workings of the thesis.
For more detail, the mmWave Radar video course by Texas Instruments\cite{ti2024lessons}, or the introductory article by \citeauthor{ti2020fundamentals}~\cite{ti2020fundamentals}, are a good starting point.

\subsection{Data Characteristics}
\label{subsection: background - millimetre wave radar - data characteristics}


%     -[[ Strengths ]]-
The data generated by these mmWave radars has some key characteristics.
% \ednote{Specify the importance of the high accuracy of the movement.\\ Explicitly name the references.}
We already mentioned the ability to sense sub-millimetre movements using phase information, which is crucial for specific applications, such as vital sign monitoring~\cite{wang2023here} and acoustic reconstruction~\cite{li2025acoustic}.
% \cite{wang2022heartprint, wang2023here, lin2024highquality, li2025acoustic}.
% One, which was already briefly mentioned, is the ability to perceive sub-millimetre distances and movements by using the phase information. 
% This ability to measure movements "as small as a fraction of a millimetre" \cite[p.~2]{ti2020fundamentals} is crucial in some applications.
Another unique feature of mmWave radars is their ability to penetrate, and thus look through, specific materials.
These materials include fabrics, such as cotton and leather \cite{meier2020propagation}, but also include solid materials, such as plastic and wood \cite{kapilevich2011fmcw}.
This allows for seamless installation behind otherwise solid structures (such as a plastic case), and can help in the detection of hidden objects and contraband.


%     -[[ Pointclouds ]]
mmWave radar is also able to output several types of data, using different firmwares.
% A lot of different data can be gathered from the mmWave radar, which all depends on the application.
% \ednote{This sentence is not about mmWave but about the thesis.\\ "A key characteristic, which is important for this thesis, is that a majority of firmwares produce point clouds."}
The majority of these firmwares, however, produce point clouds as output, which are what this thesis aims to interpret.
% However, for this thesis, we will only use the point clouds, which are generated in the majority of firmwares for the mmWave radar.
%TI provides multiple sample firmwares for its mmWave radars, specialised for different tasks; however, almost all of them generate point clouds.
These point clouds generated by the mmWave radar are relatively sparse, generating around 10 frames per second, usually with no more than 30 points per frame, dropping to unusably low levels in some frames, see \cref{subfig: sparse pointcloud}.
Besides just sparse frames, the mmWave radar also regularly ($\sim10\%$) produces \textit{partial frames} where a part of the point cloud seems to be missing, see \cref{subfig: incomplete pointcloud}.
The point clouds produced by the mmWave radar are not only sparse, but also quite noisy \cite{brescia2023millinoise}.
This noise occurs in two distinct manners: low-density consistent \textit{background noise}, and \textit{high-density single-frame} noise spikes (see \cref{subfig: dense pointcloud}).
The above-mentioned issues mean that the consistency and stability across frames are very low, which poses a challenge to data interpretation.

Setups which negate one of these issues, such as the low density of the point clouds, do exist, but they suffer in other aspects, such as creating significantly more noise points.
Different firmwares have to make concessions with regard to point-density, accuracy and the signal-to-noise ratio.
Furthermore, external factors also affect the output quality of the point cloud, such as the materials in the frame or the characteristics of the room in which the radar operates.
